# 04_CLAIM_EVIDENCE_CONTRACT.md

## Status

**BINDING CLAIM–EVIDENCE CONTRACT — 2026-10-06**

This file governs what the manuscript is allowed to claim from the evidence currently available.

It exists to prevent:
- causal inflation;
- inferred intention;
- political overstatement;
- source drift;
- silent upgrades from interpretation to historical fact.

This contract applies to all manuscript edits.

---

# 1. Claim classes

Every consequential sentence should be understood as one of the following:

## D — Descriptive
States what a source, institution, series, event, or text contains or reports.

Examples:
- a survey used a particular benchmark;
- the Fed published a given series;
- an author defined capacity in a particular way.

Evidence threshold: **low to moderate**, but source accuracy must be exact.

---

## H — Historical interpretive
Interprets the significance of documented events or institutional practices.

Examples:
- the survey's regularity helped establish institutional authority;
- the 1973 debate reveals disagreement over what counted as meaningful capacity.

Evidence threshold: **moderate**.

Must remain distinguishable from direct statements of actor intent.

---

## M — Mechanism
Claims that one process helped produce another.

Examples:
- administrative usability contributed to institutional adoption;
- embedding in official routines created path dependence.

Evidence threshold: **moderate to high**.

Requires:
- temporal sequence;
- plausible pathway;
- evidence connecting the stages.

---

## C — Causal
Claims that X caused or materially produced Y.

Examples:
- a measurement change caused a policy shift;
- survey adoption displaced an alternative because of political pressure.

Evidence threshold: **high**.

A citation to broad secondary literature is not enough.

---

## I — Intentional / strategic
Claims that actors acted in order to achieve a purpose.

Examples:
- officials selected a series in order to discipline wages;
- economists redefined capacity to suppress full-employment demands.

Evidence threshold: **very high**.

Requires actor-specific documentary evidence.

Do not infer intent from outcome.

---

## N — Normative
States what should be done or what contemporary policy should learn.

Examples:
- measurement practices should be pluralized;
- macroeconomic indicators should be democratically contestable.

Evidence threshold:
not historical proof, but must be clearly marked as implication or recommendation.

Normative claims cannot be written as if they were empirical findings.

---

# 2. Evidence tiers

## Tier 1 — Direct primary evidence
Preferred for:
- definitions;
- chronology;
- institutional action;
- stated motives;
- policy use.

Includes:
- Brookings reports;
- Federal Reserve releases and methodological documentation;
- McGraw–Hill survey documentation;
- symposium transcripts/discussions;
- original writings by economists/policymakers.

---

## Tier 2 — Contemporary technical evidence
Preferred for:
- methodological controversy;
- contemporary evaluation;
- comparative measurement performance.

Includes:
- contemporary reviews;
- technical critiques;
- empirical comparisons;
- methodological debates.

---

## Tier 3 — Historical scholarship
Appropriate for:
- context;
- chronology;
- interpretation;
- intellectual history.

May support mechanisms when the historian explicitly documents them.

---

## Tier 4 — Political-economy synthesis
Appropriate for:
- broader historical framing;
- regime interpretation;
- contextual implications.

Tier 4 evidence **cannot by itself establish**:
- actor intent;
- direct institutional action;
- a specific measurement → policy → class-outcome mechanism.

---

# 3. Claim-strength rule

A sentence may not be stronger than the evidence supporting it.

Use the following downgrade ladder:

**designed to / intended to**  
↓  
**was used to**  
↓  
**contributed to**  
↓  
**reinforced / facilitated / enabled**  
↓  
**was compatible with / aligned with**  
↓  
**can be interpreted as**

When evidence is insufficient, move downward.

Do not add citations merely to preserve a high-burden verb.

---

# 4. Agency rule

For every important claim involving actors, distinguish:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Actor | Who acted? |
| Venue | Where did the action occur? |
| Object | What exactly was changed, selected, interpreted, or implemented? |
| Rationale | What problem did the actor say they were solving? |
| Action | What did they actually do? |
| Consequence | What changed afterward? |
| Intent status | Is intention documented, inferred, or unknown? |

If any field is unknown, the prose must reflect that uncertainty.

---

# 5. "Recoding" contract

"Recoding" is authorized only when the manuscript specifies:

1. the object being recoded;
2. the prior interpretation;
3. the later interpretation;
4. the actors or institutions involved;
5. the evidence demonstrating the change.

Preferred object:
**the policy interpretation / policy-use regime surrounding capacity-utilization measurement.**

Do not use "recoding" as a metaphor for a broad political transition with no direct measurement evidence.

---

# 6. Institutional-conversion contract

"Institutional conversion" may be used only if:

- a continuing institutional structure/routine can be identified;
- its formal existence persists;
- its use or meaning changes;
- the article shows the change rather than asserting it.

Do not imply that an unchanged statistical series persisted if the historical record instead shows:
- new benchmarking;
- model-based complements;
- revised definitions;
- shifts toward potential output or natural-rate concepts.

Preferred formulation:
**conversion/reorientation of an interpretive and statistical policy infrastructure.**

---

# 7. Labor-discipline gate

The following strong claims are currently **NOT CLEARED**:

- capacity utilization was an instrument of labor discipline;
- the Fed recoded utilization in order to discipline labor;
- utilization statistics defeated egalitarian claims;
- measurement reform was designed to shift power to financial capital.

These may be reinstated only if the evidence shows:

1. actor-specific use of utilization/inflation-capacity reasoning;
2. a policy action or constraint connected to that interpretation;
3. a distributive consequence or stated distributive objective;
4. a documentary bridge between the measurement and the distributive outcome.

Absent this chain, use bounded language:

> the reinterpretation of high utilization as an inflationary constraint participated in a broader policy environment in which full-employment demand management became increasingly constrained.

---

# 8. Rival-explanation requirement

Any explanation of institutional adoption must address plausible alternatives.

For survey-based CU, at minimum consider:

- coverage;
- publication regularity;
- administrative convenience;
- cost;
- timeliness;
- reproducibility;
- institutional familiarity;
- path dependence;
- technical limitations of alternatives;
- compatibility with prevailing policy routines.

Do not write:
> "the adoption was not empirical but political"

unless the paper can rule out the technical/administrative alternatives.

Preferred form:
> technical merit alone does not explain institutional authority.

# 10. Capacity terminology contract

Use the following distinctions consistently.

## Theoretical/engineering capacity
Physical or technical upper bound under stated conditions.

## Practical capacity
Maximum sustainable output under prevailing operational norms.

## Economic capacity
Output associated with an economic criterion such as cost minimization.

## Capacity utilization
Actual output relative to a specified measure of capacity.

## Potential output
A macroeconomic construct that may be based on production-function, trend, natural-rate, or other model-based concepts.

## Output gap
Difference or ratio between actual output and a specified measure of potential output.

Rule:
**capacity is not the output gap.**
**capacity utilization is not automatically equivalent to the output gap.**

When historical actors use these terms differently, mark the usage as theirs.

# 11. Source-verification flags

Any statement receiving one of these tags must be closed before submission:

- `[VERIFY-SOURCE]`
- `[VERIFY-QUOTE]`
- `[VERIFY-DATE]`
- `[VERIFY-AGENCY]`
- `[VERIFY-CAUSAL]`
- `[VERIFY-DEFINITION]`

No flagged claim may remain in the submission PDF.

Known current priority:
- Klein & David (1958) attribution and bibliographic match.

---

# 12. New-source rule

New references should be added only for an identified evidentiary or positioning gap.

Every proposed new source should be logged with:

- claim it supports;
- reason existing corpus is insufficient;
- source type;
- whether it supplies direct evidence or contextual interpretation.

Do not add references simply to make a paragraph look more authoritative.

---

# 13. Evidence-preservation rule

When revising prose:

- do not delete source-specific factual details that are carrying the argument;
- do not replace primary-source evidence with generalized theoretical prose;
- do not compress away the differences among competing capacity measures;
- do not "humanize" prose by making evidentiary qualifications disappear.

The strongest parts of the paper are evidence-dense.

Protect them.

---

# 14. Acceptance test

A major claim passes this contract only if an editor can answer:

1. What kind of claim is this?
2. What evidence tier supports it?
3. Does the verb match the evidence strength?
4. Is agency explicit where relevant?
5. Is intent documented or inferred?
6. Has the strongest rival explanation been considered?
7. Does the conclusion state the claim at the same strength as the body?

If any answer is unclear, the claim remains open.
